# 04-SHELTER — Best Building Methods for Humid Subtropical Climates (NC Focus)
*Matching structure to climate, not to romance. NC design constraints: 45–50 in/yr
rain, high summer humidity, hurricanes/tornadoes (coastal + Piedmont), termites,
hot summers, mild-but-real winters, clay-heavy soils in much of the state.*

## The NC Design Brief (what any method must survive)

1. **Water everywhere** — driving rain, ground moisture, humidity. The #1 killer
   of natural building here is unmanaged moisture, not cold
2. **Wind** — hurricane-force gusts inland are possible; coastal is certain
3. **Termites** — earth-contact wood needs separation strategies
4. **Summer heat** — shading and ventilation outrank insulation in July
5. **Mild winters** — much less insulation needed than cold climates; mass + some
   insulation wins

## Ranked Methods for NC Permaculture Communities

### ★ 1. Geodesic Dome (panel or aircrete) — the best wind-form on Earth
- **Why it fits NC:** the aerodynamics are unmatched — hurricane winds flow
  around a dome instead of pushing it over; minimal surface area = minimal
  rain exposure and minimal envelope to waterproof per unit of floor area
- **Aircrete dome route (DomeGaia-style):** aircrete blocks/panels, low cost,
  highly insulating (trapped air in foam), sculptable curved forms, one person
  can build with the "Little Dragon" foam generator
- **Panel-kit route:** engineered dome kits come with stamped engineering for
  wind loads — the easy permit path; DIY panels cheaper but need engineering
- **The honest challenges (humid climate):**
  - **Seams.** Many panels = many joints; every seam is a leak candidate in
    50 in/yr of rain. Solution: continuous membrane + elastomeric coating over
    the whole shell, inspected annually
  - **Interior condensation.** Curved single-surface shells collect
    condensation without interior ventilation planning — thermal-break the
    skin and add ridge ventilation, or the dome "rains" inside in August
  - **Furniture on curves** — plan flat wall insets (kitchen, closets) during
    design, not after
  - **Cost reality:** panel kits $40–80/sq ft; aircrete DIY $20–50/sq ft
    (materials), cheaper than code-frame but the waterproofing is not optional
- **Verdict: excellent choice** — arguably the best structural form for NC wind,
  provided you over-engineer waterproofing and ventilation

### ★ 2. Aircrete — great material, with rules
- **What it is:** foamed cement (air bubbles in slurry) — lightweight,
  insulating (R ~1.5–2/inch), fire/termite/rot proof, castable into any form,
  ~$0.50–2 per block equivalent material cost
- **Best NC uses:** dome shells, monolithic cast walls, insulation layers over
  thermal mass, foundation stem walls
- **The limits (why it isn't magic):**
  - **Compressive strength is modest** (~300–800 psi DIY densities) — fine for
    domes and single-story, not for point loads or two stories without design
  - **Needs coating:** bare cementitious surfaces wick and hold moisture in
    humid climates — plaster/stucco + good roof overhang discipline required
  - **Quality control:** bad batches (wrong foam ratio) crumble; test casts
    before committing a season to it
- **Code path:** alternative-materials provision, engineer stamp — same as
  earthbag/earthship, and aircrete's predictability makes engineers MORE willing
  to stamp it than rammed tires

### ★ 3. Insulated earth-sheltered rectilinear (the sleeper pick)
- Earth-bermed walls on 3 sides + conventional framed/code-compliant south face
- **Why it may beat domes for NC:** south glazing is awkward on a geodesic
  (triangles everywhere); the rectilinear earth-berm gives you big south glass
  for passive solar, berm dirt from pad excavation (cut-and-fill again), and the
  EASIEST permit path of anything on this list
- Pairs with earth tubes + berm = near-domes performance, near-zero permit friction

### 4. Earthbag (superadobe) domes
- Proven (Cal-Earth), wind-resistant curves, dirt-cheap materials
- **NC caveat:** same thermal-mass-in-humid-ground problem as earthships —
  must insulate outside the bag shell (R-10 wrap under plaster), and the
  permit path needs engineering. Slightly harder to stamp than aircrete

### 5. Cob / adobe — romantic but wrong biome
- Mass walls without insulation = NC summers cook you from stored daytime heat
- Fine for interior feature walls and benches; wrong primary structure here

### 6. Skip/avoid in NC
- Straw bale with poor roof overhangs (moisture risk at 50 in/yr — doable with
  big overhangs but demands discipline)
- Unprotected earth-plastered anything on weather faces
- Wood-earth contact without termite separation details

## The Recommended NC Hybrid (what the kernel would actually build)

**Geodesic or aircrete dome ON a raised, drained platform, with:**
- Gravel/rubble-trench or aircrete stem foundation — capillary break, termite shield
- Full continuous waterproof membrane + light-colored elastomeric coating (shed summer heat AND water)
- South-facing glazing bay (rectilinear entry/greenhouse wing off the dome —
  solves both the furniture problem and passive solar)
- Earth tubes on the north side (see site-shaping.md)
- Ridge/venturi ventilation planned from day one — domes need deliberate airflow design
- Rain catchment sized for 50 in/yr — cisterns are the cheapest utility in NC
- Permaculture integration: dome sits high on the contour, berm courts on north,
  greenhouse glazing wing doubles as the greywater botanical cell (legal, since
  greywater-only with an approved blackwater septic — see earthship doc)

**Cost reality check (materials, DIY labor):**
- Aircrete dome 600 sq ft: ~$15–30k materials
- Panel-kit dome: ~$35–60k
- Earth-berm hybrid: variable, often cheapest per sq ft
- All three: engineer stamp $2–5k, septic $6–12k, well or cistern system $3–8k
